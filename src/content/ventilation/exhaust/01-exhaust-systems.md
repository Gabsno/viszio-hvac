Exhaust systems remove contaminated, hot or hazardous air from spaces where it is generated and discharge it safely outdoors. Unlike comfort ventilation, which dilutes air for people, exhaust is about capturing a problem at source and getting rid of it.

## General exhaust for toilets and washrooms

Toilets, washrooms and shower rooms generate odour and moisture. They are ventilated by general exhaust — an extract fan and duct system that continuously pulls air out of the space and discharges it outside, while replacement air transfers in from the surrounding corridors through door undercuts or transfer grilles.

The room is intentionally held at negative pressure so odour and moist air are contained and cannot drift back into offices or circulation areas. There is usually no dedicated supply to the toilet itself; the corridor supply system provides the make up air.

Rates are commonly set on an air change basis or per fixture. Typical design figures land around 10 to 15 air changes per hour for toilet rooms, or roughly 10 to 12 L/s per square metre of floor, with the higher end for busy public facilities. Exhaust fans for toilets are often run continuously during occupied hours, or switched with lighting and occupancy in smaller installations. Ducts should fall toward drainage points where condensation can collect in humid climates.

## Car park ventilation

Enclosed car parks need ventilation for two distinct duties, and the system is sized for the more demanding of them.

### Carbon monoxide control

Idling and manoeuvring vehicles release carbon monoxide, a colourless, odourless and toxic gas. The ventilation system dilutes it to a safe concentration. Carbon monoxide sensors distributed through the car park modulate the fans — running them slow or off when the space is quiet, ramping up when a sensor detects rising concentration. This demand based control saves substantial fan energy compared with running flat out.

Typical car park ventilation is sized in the region of 6 to 10 air changes per hour, with the exact figure set by the governing code and the car park geometry. Both supply and exhaust, or jet fan induction systems, are used to ensure no stagnant pockets remain.

### Smoke clearance

The second duty is smoke clearance in a fire — moving smoke out so the fire service can locate and fight a vehicle fire. Smoke clearance demands a higher airflow than carbon monoxide dilution and a high temperature rated fan that keeps running in hot smoke. The fans switch to a boost or emergency mode on a fire signal. Jet fan systems, which use small thrust fans to sweep air across the floor plate toward extract points, are popular because they avoid extensive ductwork.

## Battery generator and plant room exhaust

Equipment rooms each have a specific hazard the exhaust must address.

| Room | Hazard | Exhaust purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Battery room | Hydrogen gas, explosive | Dilute hydrogen below the flammable limit |
| Generator room | Engine heat, exhaust fumes, fuel vapour | Remove heat, supply combustion air |
| Electrical switch room | Equipment heat | Limit temperature rise |
| Transformer room | Heat | Limit temperature rise |
| Chemical or fuel store | Vapour | Contain and remove vapour |

Battery rooms holding flooded lead acid batteries release hydrogen during charging. Hydrogen is explosive above about 4 percent in air, so the exhaust is sized to keep concentration well below that, ventilation runs continuously, and switchgear in the room should be non sparking. Generator rooms are dominated by heat — a running engine rejects large amounts of heat to the room — and the ventilation must remove that heat to keep intake air temperatures within the engine manufacturer limit, while also supplying combustion air for the engine itself. Switch and transformer rooms are simpler heat removal problems, sized so the room temperature rise stays within the equipment rating.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Standby generators are near universal in Ghanaian buildings because of grid reliability, so generator and battery room ventilation deserves real attention rather than a token fan. Size generator room exhaust for the full engine heat rejection at Accra ambient temperatures, which are high, and confirm adequate combustion air. Ventilate battery rooms and fuel storage areas continuously, keep ignition sources out, and fit gas detection where flammable vapour is credible.

## Typical air change rates

Air changes per hour is the usual sizing basis for exhaust spaces, because the contaminant relates to the space rather than to occupants. As a starting point:

| Space | Typical air changes per hour |
|---|---|
| Toilet washroom | 10 to 15 |
| Enclosed car park | 6 to 10 |
| Battery room | 6 to 12, plus hydrogen check |
| Generator room | High, set by heat removal |
| Electrical switch room | 4 to 10, set by heat |
| Store room | 2 to 6 |

These are starting points only. The governing code, the manufacturer data and the actual heat or contaminant load always override a rule of thumb.

## Interlocks and gas detection

Exhaust systems are protected and controlled by interlocks and detectors so they do the right thing automatically.

Carbon monoxide sensors run car park fans on demand and trigger boost mode at high concentration. Hydrogen detectors in battery rooms can alarm and force ventilation to maximum. Generator room fans and louvres are interlocked to open and run whenever the engine runs. Smoke detection switches car park and other smoke control fans into emergency mode and overrides normal energy saving control. Where a room has both supply and exhaust, the two are interlocked so make up air is always available and the fan is never starved.

A good exhaust design also fails safe: on loss of control signal or sensor, the safety critical fans default to running rather than stopping.

## Practical takeaways

Treat each exhaust space on its own terms — identify the contaminant or hazard, size the airflow to control it, and pick a fan rated for the duty including high temperature service where smoke is involved. Provide make up air, keep contaminant rooms negative, and wire in the gas detection and interlocks that make the system respond on its own. Commission the airflows and the sensor setpoints, and verify them again after any change to the building.
