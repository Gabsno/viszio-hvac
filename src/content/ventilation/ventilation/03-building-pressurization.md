Building pressurization is the control of indoor air pressure relative to the outdoors and between adjacent spaces, achieved by deliberately balancing how much air is supplied against how much is exhausted. It governs which way air leaks, and getting it right keeps dust, humidity, odours and smoke where they belong.

## How pressure is created

Air moves from higher pressure to lower pressure. A room is at positive pressure when more air is supplied into it than is removed, so the surplus leaks outward through cracks, gaps and door undercuts. It is at negative pressure when more air is removed than supplied, so air leaks inward to make up the deficit.

The pressure itself is small. Building pressure differences are measured in pascals, and useful working values are only a few pascals — typically 5 to 15 Pa between a space and its surroundings. For comparison, atmospheric pressure is about 101325 Pa, so these are tiny offsets. They are small because building envelopes and partitions are leaky; pushing pressure higher just forces more air through the leaks without much benefit, and makes doors hard to use.

The lever the designer controls is the supply minus exhaust offset. A space supplied with 1000 L/s and exhausted at 850 L/s has a 150 L/s positive offset that must leave through the envelope and transfer paths. Reverse the numbers and the space goes negative by the same amount.

## Positive pressure to keep contaminants out

Most occupied commercial space is run slightly positive overall. The supply air handlers deliver a little more conditioned air than the exhaust and relief systems remove, and the surplus leaks outward.

The benefit is that infiltration is suppressed. Instead of hot, dusty, humid outdoor air sneaking in through window frames, door gaps and facade joints — uncontrolled and unconditioned — the leakage flows the other way, outward, carrying filtered conditioned air. This protects indoor humidity control, keeps airborne dust out, reduces drafts and helps the air handlers maintain stable conditions. Clean rooms, electronics areas and data halls use stronger positive pressure for the same reason, cascading from cleanest to least clean.

The offset is modest and deliberate. Too much positive pressure makes exterior doors hard to pull shut and whistles air through every gap.

## Negative pressure to contain contaminants

Some rooms must be the opposite. Toilets and washrooms, commercial kitchens, laboratories, soiled linen and refuse rooms, battery rooms and chemical stores are run at negative pressure so air flows into them from surrounding spaces and never out of them.

This is achieved by exhausting more than is supplied. The room may receive little or no dedicated supply; instead, transfer air enters under the door or through a transfer grille from the corridor, is contaminated by the room, and is removed by the exhaust fan. Odours, moisture, fumes and aerosols are captured at source and discharged outdoors rather than allowed to migrate into clean occupied areas.

| Space | Pressure regime | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Office and lobby | Slightly positive | Keep dust and humidity out |
| Clean room and data hall | Strongly positive | Exclude particulate |
| Toilet and washroom | Negative | Contain odour and moisture |
| Commercial kitchen | Negative | Contain heat grease and smell |
| Laboratory and chemical store | Negative | Contain fumes |

The whole building is a network. A floor that is positive overall still contains negative pockets, and the make up air for those exhausts has to come from somewhere — usually the positive offset of the surrounding supply system. Supply, exhaust and transfer paths are designed together.

## Stairwell pressurization for life safety

Stairwell pressurization is a fire and smoke control measure, not a comfort one. In a fire, the protected escape stairs and lift lobbies must stay clear of smoke so occupants can evacuate and firefighters can ascend.

A dedicated pressurization fan, started by the fire alarm, blows outdoor air into the stair shaft to hold it at positive pressure relative to the adjacent floors. Smoke, which is at the floor pressure, cannot then leak into the stair through door gaps because the airflow opposes it. A common design target is on the order of 25 to 50 Pa across the closed stair door, with the exact figures set by the governing fire code.

The system is sized for two competing conditions. With all doors closed it must not let pressure climb so high that the doors cannot be opened. With several doors open — the realistic evacuation case — it must still move enough air through the open doorways to keep a minimum velocity sweeping smoke back. Relief dampers, variable speed fans or barometric relief manage the swing between these states.

## Door opening force limits

Pressurization runs straight into a human factors limit. If the pressure across a door is too high, an occupant — possibly elderly, injured or a child — cannot pull it open against the force, and an escape door that will not open is a fatal hazard.

The force a person exerts at the handle to overcome the pressure differential, plus the door closer force, must stay within a code limit. A widely used ceiling is about 133 newtons of total opening force at the handle, with the pressure component a portion of that. This caps the practical pressure difference across an escape door and is why stairwell systems need relief: the fan must hold smoke out without locking people in.

## Commissioning the result

Pressurization only works if it is verified. Measure pressure differentials room to room and across stair doors with a manometer, confirm door opening forces with a force gauge, and check the supply and exhaust offsets with airflow readings. Balance the system as a network, document the offsets, and re commission after any change to the air handling or exhaust systems — pressurization drifts the moment the airflow balance is disturbed.
