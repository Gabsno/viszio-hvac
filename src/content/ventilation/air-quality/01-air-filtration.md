Air filtration removes particles from an airstream as it passes through a fibrous or pleated medium. Where ventilation dilutes contaminants, filtration actually captures and holds them, protecting both the building and the people inside it.

## Why filtration matters

Filters do three jobs at once. They protect the HVAC equipment, they keep the ductwork clean, and they protect occupants.

Equipment protection is often underrated. A cooling coil is a dense matrix of fins; airborne dust settling on a wet coil binds into a felt like layer that insulates the heat transfer surface, raises air pressure drop and starves the coil of airflow. A fouled coil runs warmer, dehumidifies poorly and costs more to operate, and it is awkward and expensive to clean. Good filtration keeps the coil, the fan and the heating elements clean and performing as designed.

Ductwork protection follows the same logic. Dust that gets past a weak filter settles along duct walls, becomes a reservoir for dirt and microbial growth, and is eventually re entrained into the supply air.

Occupant protection is the health driver. Fine particles, especially the fraction small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs, are linked to respiratory and cardiovascular harm. Effective filtration of supply and recirculated air reduces the indoor concentration of these particles.

## The MERV rating scale

MERV, the Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value, is the standard North American scale for general ventilation filters. It runs from MERV 1 to MERV 16. A higher number means the filter captures a larger fraction of smaller particles.

The rating is based on how efficiently a filter removes particles in three size ranges, with the small particle range being the demanding test. Low MERV filters only catch coarse dust, lint and hair. Mid range filters start to capture the fine particles that matter for health. High MERV filters approach, but do not reach, the performance of a true HEPA filter.

| MERV range | Captures | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| 1 to 4 | Coarse dust, lint, hair | Minimal, equipment guard only |
| 5 to 8 | Mould spores, fine dust | Basic commercial, pre filter |
| 9 to 12 | Fine particles, some smoke | Good commercial offices |
| 13 to 16 | Fine respirable particles | Hospitals, high IAQ buildings |

A general office is well served by MERV 13 as the final filter; it captures a high fraction of respirable particles without an extreme pressure drop. Many codes and guidance documents now treat MERV 13 as a sensible minimum for occupied commercial space.

### The ISO ePM equivalents

International practice uses ISO 16890, which classifies filters by how well they capture defined particle size fractions. The ePM1, ePM2.5 and ePM10 grades state the percentage of particles up to 1, 2.5 and 10 micrometres that the filter removes. The ePM2.5 grade aligns with the air quality particle metric that health authorities track.

There is no exact one to one conversion between MERV and ISO ePM, but as a working guide MERV 13 corresponds roughly to about ePM1 50 to 60 percent, and MERV 14 to ePM1 70 to 80 percent. Always check the manufacturer data rather than relying on a conversion chart.

## HEPA filters and where they are needed

HEPA, the High Efficiency Particulate Air filter, is a different class of product. A true HEPA filter removes at least 99.97 percent of particles at the most penetrating particle size, around 0.3 micrometres. Its pleated glass fibre medium is deep, dense and has a high pressure drop.

HEPA is not a general ventilation filter and should not be specified as one. It belongs in cleanrooms, operating theatres, isolation rooms, pharmaceutical production, laboratory containment and similar applications where the particle requirement is absolute. Putting a HEPA filter into an ordinary air handler designed for MERV 13 will choke the airflow because the fan was never sized for the pressure drop.

## Pre filter and final filter staging

Filters are arranged in stages, and staging is what makes a high efficiency system economical.

A bare high efficiency final filter exposed to the full dust load clogs quickly and is costly to replace. Placing a cheaper, lower grade pre filter upstream — typically a MERV 7 or 8 panel — catches the coarse and medium dust first. The expensive final filter then sees only the fine residual load, so it lasts far longer.

A HEPA installation usually runs two stages of protection ahead of the HEPA itself: a coarse pre filter and a mid grade secondary filter, so the HEPA, the most expensive element by far, is changed least often. The principle is simple — protect the costly filter with the cheap one.

## Pressure drop filter life and fan energy

Every filter resists airflow, and that resistance is a real operating cost. A clean filter has an initial pressure drop; as it loads with dust the pressure drop rises until it reaches the final or change out value, typically two to three times the clean figure.

The fan must overcome that pressure drop, and fan power rises with it. Over a filter service life the energy spent pushing air through a dirty filter can exceed the purchase price of the filter several times over. Higher MERV grades cost more energy as well as more money.

Three practical rules follow. First, choose the filter grade the application genuinely needs — MERV 13 for a normal office, higher only where justified. Second, change filters on measured pressure drop using a differential pressure gauge or sensor across the bank, not on a fixed calendar, so a filter is neither thrown away clean nor run far past its limit. Third, size the air handler and fan for the dirty filter condition, so the system still delivers design airflow near the end of filter life.

## Practical takeaways

Filtration protects coils, ducts and lungs, and it is cheap insurance compared with the cost of a fouled coil or poor indoor air quality. Pick the MERV or ISO ePM grade the building needs, stage a pre filter ahead of the final filter, never substitute a HEPA into an ordinary handler, and manage filter changes on pressure drop. Account for the fan energy of filtration in the design — it is a recurring cost for the life of the building.
