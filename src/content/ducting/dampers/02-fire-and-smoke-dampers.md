Fire and smoke dampers are life-safety devices that keep a duct penetration from becoming the weak point in a rated barrier. They are not air-control devices, and getting their selection and placement wrong is a failed inspection at best and a spread of fire at worst.

## Fire dampers

A fire damper restores the fire resistance of a wall or floor where a duct passes through it. Under normal conditions it is held fully open and lets air pass. When the air temperature reaches a set value — commonly around 74 °C (165 °F) — a fusible link melts and a spring-driven curtain or set of blades snaps shut, blocking the opening and re-establishing the barrier.

Fire dampers are tested and listed to a fire-resistance rating that must match the barrier.

| Damper rating | Typical barrier rated for |
|---|---|
| 1.5 hour | Walls and partitions rated less than 3 hours |
| 3 hour | Walls and floors rated 3 hours or more |

A higher fusible-link temperature is used where the duct normally carries warm air, so the link does not nuisance-trip. The damper sleeve, retaining angles and the gap to the structure must follow the listing exactly — an oversize annular space or the wrong fastening voids the rating. **Static** fire dampers are listed for systems that shut down in a fire; **dynamic** fire dampers are listed to close against a running fan and a stated airflow and pressure, and are required wherever the fan may keep operating.

## Smoke dampers

A smoke damper limits the movement of smoke through a duct or an opening in a smoke barrier. Unlike a fire damper it does not wait for heat — it closes on a signal, typically from a duct or area smoke detector or from the fire alarm and building management system. Smoke dampers carry a **leakage rating** (Class I, II or III, Class I being the tightest) and an elevated-temperature rating, because a smoke control strategy depends on the damper actually sealing.

Smoke dampers are actuator-driven and can be commanded as part of a smoke-control sequence — closing some dampers while others open to pressurize stairs or exhaust a fire floor.

## Combination fire and smoke dampers

A combination fire and smoke damper does both jobs in one listed assembly. It closes on heat through a thermal element and also closes on a smoke or alarm signal through an actuator, and it carries both a fire-resistance rating and a leakage rating. Combination dampers are the usual choice where a duct crosses a barrier that is both fire rated and a smoke barrier, which is common at shaft walls and along exit corridors. They cost more and need power and control wiring, but they avoid stacking two separate devices in one penetration.

## Ceiling radiation backdraft and gravity dampers

- **Ceiling radiation dampers** protect openings in a fire-rated floor-ceiling or roof-ceiling assembly — for example, where a diffuser sits in a rated membrane ceiling. They limit radiant heat transfer through the opening and are listed specifically for that assembly type; an ordinary fire damper is not a substitute.
- **Backdraft dampers** are light, low-leakage gravity or spring devices that allow airflow in one direction only. They stop exhausted air, outdoor wind or another fan's discharge from flowing back through an idle fan.
- **Gravity dampers** open under the system's own airflow and fall shut by their own weight when the fan stops, commonly at intake and relief louvers.

These are not rated barrier devices — do not use a backdraft or gravity damper where a fire or smoke damper is required.

## Where the mechanical code requires them

The mechanical code — IMC Section 607 in jurisdictions that adopt the International Mechanical Code — sets out where each damper type is mandatory. The principle is simple: any duct that breaches a rated assembly must not defeat that assembly.

Typical requirements include:

- **Fire dampers** where ducts or air-transfer openings penetrate fire-rated walls, fire barriers, fire partitions and rated floor or floor-ceiling assemblies.
- **Smoke dampers** where ducts or transfer openings penetrate smoke barriers, and at openings serving smoke-control or pressurization systems.
- **Combination fire and smoke dampers** where the penetrated assembly is both fire rated and a smoke barrier, such as shaft enclosures and corridor smoke barriers.
- **Ceiling radiation dampers** at duct and air-handling openings in rated ceiling membranes.

The code also lists narrow exceptions — for example, certain fully ducted, low-area penetrations or steel sub-ducts extending up in a vented shaft — but exceptions are specific and must be checked against the adopted edition, not assumed.

## Access doors and resetting

Every fire, smoke and combination damper must have an **access door** large enough to inspect the damper, verify the blades and fusible link, exercise the actuator and reset the device after a trip or a test. The access panel must be permanently and clearly labelled so it is not hidden by later finishes, and it must be reachable — not buried above an inaccessible hard ceiling. Local fire codes require periodic operational testing of these dampers over the building's life, and a damper that cannot be reached cannot be tested or recommissioned. Coordinate access door locations with the ceiling and architectural team early.

## The grease duct rule

One prohibition is absolute: **fire dampers are not permitted in grease ducts.** A Type I kitchen exhaust grease duct must run unobstructed and liquid-tight from the hood to the exterior so grease drains and so the duct can be cleaned along its full length. A fire damper inside a grease duct would collect grease, could jam, and would itself become an ignition point. NFPA 96 and the mechanical code prohibit dampers of any kind in grease ducts; protect the grease duct instead with welded enclosure construction, listed clearances and the hood's wet-chemical suppression system.

Match the damper rating to the barrier, choose the right type for heat versus smoke versus combined duty, provide labelled access at every device, and keep all dampers out of grease ducts.
