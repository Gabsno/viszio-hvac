The air terminal is the last component the conditioned air passes through, and it decides whether the occupant feels comfort or a draught. Good duct design is wasted if the wrong terminal dumps cold air on someone's neck.

## Diffuser grille and register

These three words are used loosely on site, but they describe distinct devices.

A **grille** is a faced opening with fixed or adjustable bars or louvres. It covers an opening and directs air in a broad pattern, but it has no damper. Grilles are common on return and exhaust openings, and as supply grilles on sidewalls.

A **register** is a grille with a built-in **damper** behind it. The damper, usually an opposed-blade type operated by a key or lever, lets the airflow through that single outlet be throttled. So: grille plus damper equals register.

A **diffuser** is a supply terminal designed to spread air in a deliberate pattern — most often a ceiling device with concentric cones or slots that throw air horizontally across the ceiling. Its job is not just to pass air but to mix it with room air gently so no draught is felt.

| Device | Has damper | Spreads air in a pattern | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Grille | no | basic, directional | return, exhaust, sidewall supply |
| Register | yes | basic, directional | supply where local throttling is wanted |
| Diffuser | sometimes | yes, engineered | ceiling supply |

## Supply return and exhaust terminals

Terminals fall into three duties and must not be swapped.

**Supply** terminals deliver conditioned air into the room. They are selected for a pattern that mixes the supply air with room air before it reaches the occupied zone. Ceiling diffusers, linear slot diffusers and supply grilles do this work.

**Return** terminals collect room air to send back to the air handler. Their job is simply to pass air with low resistance and low noise; they need no throw pattern, because air approaches a return opening from all directions and the velocity falls off fast. Return grilles can therefore be larger and simpler.

**Exhaust** terminals remove air from the building entirely — from toilets, kitchens, plant rooms. They look like return grilles but serve a different airflow path.

A return grille used as a supply outlet will not mix the air and will dump it; a supply diffuser used as a return wastes money on a pattern that does nothing. Match the terminal to the duty.

## Throw drop and spread

Three terms describe how a supply jet behaves, and selection turns on them.

- **Throw** is the horizontal distance the air jet travels from the diffuser before its velocity decays to a stated terminal velocity, commonly 0.25 m/s (50 fpm). It tells you how far the diffuser reaches.
- **Drop** is how far the jet falls vertically as it travels. A cold jet is denser than room air and tends to drop; too much drop puts cold fast air into the occupied zone.
- **Spread** is how wide the jet fans out from the diffuser.

The aim is to size and select a diffuser so its throw reaches roughly to the wall or to the next diffuser's throw — neither falling short nor blasting past — while keeping drop small enough that the jet has slowed and mixed before it descends into the space people occupy.

## The Coanda effect and ceiling attachment

A horizontal jet leaving a ceiling diffuser does not immediately fall. It clings to the ceiling surface and runs along it. This is the **Coanda effect**: the jet entrains room air on its open side, a slight low pressure forms between the jet and the ceiling, and that pressure holds the jet against the ceiling.

Coanda attachment is the designer's friend. While the jet hugs the ceiling it stays out of the occupied zone, mixing with room air and shedding velocity and temperature difference. By the time it leaves the ceiling and descends, it is slow and close to room temperature — comfortable.

The effect breaks down if the diffuser is overloaded, if it is mounted away from the ceiling, or if a beam or obstruction interrupts the jet. When the jet **separates** from the ceiling early, it falls as a fast cold stream — and that is felt as a draught.

## Avoiding draughts and dumping

**Dumping** is cold supply air falling straight down out of a diffuser instead of throwing across the ceiling. It happens when a diffuser is run well below its design airflow: the jet is too weak to stay attached, Coanda fails, and the cold dense air drops. Oversizing diffusers, or starving them at part load, both cause it. The remedy is to select the diffuser so it still has enough jet momentum at minimum airflow to stay attached.

A **draught** is felt air movement that is unwanted. Comfort standards treat noticeable air speeds in the occupied zone as a defect. Keep occupied-zone velocity low — generally under about 0.25 m/s — by getting throw, drop and Coanda right, and by not aiming supply jets directly at desks or seats.

## Noise criteria

Air moving through a terminal makes noise, and the faster it moves the louder it is. Manufacturers publish a sound rating, often an NC (Noise Criterion) value, for each diffuser at each airflow.

Set a target NC for the space — roughly NC 30 to 35 for an open office, lower for a meeting room or bedroom — and select diffusers that stay at or below it at design airflow. The cheap way to hit a noise target is to use a slightly larger diffuser so the face velocity drops; noise rises steeply with velocity, so a small size increase buys a large noise reduction.

## Selection from manufacturer data

A diffuser is not designed from scratch — it is selected from the manufacturer's catalogue data. For a given model and size the catalogue lists, against airflow, the throw at a stated terminal velocity, the pressure drop, and the NC rating.

The selection routine is straightforward. Take the airflow the room needs. Pick a model suited to the ceiling and pattern wanted. Choose a size whose throw matches the room geometry, whose pressure drop fits the system's available static pressure, and whose NC stays under the target. If one size fails a criterion, step up or down a size and re-check. The terminal is correct only when airflow, throw, pressure drop and noise are all satisfied at once.
