Every air system that brings in fresh air does so through an outdoor air intake, and the louvre on that intake is the building's first line of defence against rain, dust, insects and bad air. A poorly chosen louvre lets water and grime straight into the air handler.

## The outdoor air intake louvre

An outdoor air intake louvre is the weatherproof grille set in the building facade or on the roof where ventilation air enters. It has to do several jobs at once: pass the required airflow at low resistance, keep rain and windblown water out, exclude birds and insects, and look acceptable as part of the facade.

A louvre is a frame holding a set of angled blades. The blade angle and profile, the spacing, and the depth of the frame all decide how much water it stops and how much pressure it costs. The intake louvre is not interchangeable with an exhaust or relief louvre — it is selected specifically for keeping weather *out*.

## Rain and water carry over

The core challenge is stopping rain from being drawn through the louvre and into the duct behind it.

Water reaches the blades two ways: as falling rain, and as windblown droplets pushed horizontally against the facade. A plain louvre intercepts some of it, but at higher face velocity the airstream picks the water back up off the blades and carries it through — this is **water carry-over** or penetration.

The defence is the **drainable louvre**. Its blades are shaped with gutters or channels that catch intercepted water and run it down to drain pockets in the frame jambs, then out through weep holes — instead of letting it pool and re-entrain. For exposed or storm-prone locations a **storm-rated** or twin-bank louvre adds a second stage that strips out almost all carry-over even at higher velocity. Manufacturers publish a rating: the airflow at which the louvre begins to pass water. Selecting below that point is what keeps the air handler and the duct downstream dry.

## Bird screens and insect mesh

Behind the louvre blades sits a screen, and its job is to keep living things out of the duct.

A **bird screen** is a coarse mesh — galvanised or stainless, with openings around 12 to 20 mm — that stops birds and large debris. Most intake louvres should have one as standard.

An **insect mesh** is a much finer screen that also excludes flies and mosquitoes. It is valuable, but it has a cost: a fine mesh adds pressure drop and clogs with dust far faster than a bird screen, so it needs regular cleaning or it will throttle the airflow. Where insect mesh is specified, plan for access and a cleaning schedule.

## Free area and face velocity

Two numbers govern louvre sizing.

The **free area** is the open area between the blades and screen through which air actually passes — always smaller than the overall louvre size, because blades and frame block part of it. A typical intake louvre has a free area of roughly 40 to 60 percent of its gross area; the manufacturer states the exact figure.

The **face velocity** is the airflow divided by the gross louvre area; the velocity through the free area is higher still. Face velocity is the key selection variable, because water carry-over rises sharply with it.

| Face velocity m/s | Approx fpm | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| up to 2.0 | up to 400 | low carry-over, conservative, quiet |
| 2.0 to 2.5 | 400 to 500 | typical for a good drainable louvre |
| above 3.0 | above 600 | carry-over and noise rise quickly |

The design rule is simple: keep the face velocity below the louvre's published water-penetration point, with margin. If the calculated velocity is too high, the louvre is too small — make it larger so more gross area shares the airflow.

## Intake location

A clean intake matters as much as a good louvre. Drawing in contaminated air defeats the whole point of ventilation, so ventilation standards such as ASHRAE 62.1 require the intake to be separated from sources of pollution.

Keep the intake well away from:

- **building exhaust outlets** — toilet, kitchen, general extract — so used air is not re-ingested
- **plumbing and sanitary vents**
- **loading bays, driveways and car parks** where vehicle exhaust collects
- **cooling towers**, whose drift and plume carry moisture and biological risk
- **flues, generators and refuse stores**

The standard sets minimum separation distances and notes that prevailing wind can carry a plume from an exhaust to an intake even when they look far apart. Intakes are also kept above grade so they do not pick up ground-level dust, snow or pooled water. Locate the intake on the clean side of the building, upwind of contaminant sources where the site allows.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Accra's tropical storms drive heavy, wind-blown rain against building facades, and the dry harmattan season loads the air with fine dust. Both make a low intake face velocity and a genuine drainable, storm-rated louvre worth the extra cost — a cheap louvre run too fast will pass water into the air handler. Plan for routine cleaning of bird screens and any insect mesh, because harmattan dust clogs fine screens fast and a blocked intake starves the system of fresh air.
