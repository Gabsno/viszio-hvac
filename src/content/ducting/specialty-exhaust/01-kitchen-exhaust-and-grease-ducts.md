Commercial kitchen exhaust is one of the most safety-critical systems an HVAC engineer designs. The duct carries airborne grease that can ignite, so the rules are stricter than for any ordinary exhaust. This article covers hoods, grease ducts, fans, make-up air and fire protection.

## Type I and Type II hoods

The hood type follows what the cooking equipment produces.

| Hood type | Serves | Removes | Grease rated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Type I | Ranges, fryers, griddles, charbroilers, woks | Grease-laden vapour, smoke, heat | Yes |
| Type II | Dishwashers, ovens, steamers, kettles | Heat, steam, condensation, odour only | No |

A **Type I** hood is required over any appliance that produces grease or smoke. It has listed grease-extraction baffle filters, a grease gutter and cup, and it discharges into a fully grease-rated duct system. A **Type II** hood handles only heat and moisture and may use a lighter duct, but it must never serve grease-producing equipment. When in doubt, the appliance's listing or the cooking process — anything that fries, grills or chars — drives a Type I hood.

## Capture velocity and hood overhang

A hood only works if it captures the rising plume before room air currents pull it away. Two design levers control this.

- **Exhaust airflow** sets the face and capture velocity. Wall-mounted canopy hoods are typically designed around 250 to 400 L/s per metre of hood length (roughly 150 to 300 CFM per foot), with charbroilers and solid-fuel appliances at the higher end and light-duty equipment lower. Manufacturers' listed flow rates for the specific hood and appliance line govern.
- **Overhang.** The hood should extend beyond the cooking surface on all open sides — commonly about 150 mm (6 in) minimum, and more for heavy-duty appliances — so the plume is caught even as it spreads while rising.

Keep hot appliances back from open hood edges, limit cross-draughts from doors and supply diffusers, and never blow a supply air jet across the face of a hood.

## Grease duct rules

The grease duct is the heart of the fire risk and has the strictest rules in HVAC. Grease condenses on the duct walls; if it ignites, the duct must contain the fire and not feed it.

- **Welded liquid-tight construction.** Grease ducts are built from steel with continuous liquid-tight welds — no slip joints, no sealant-only seams. Listed grease duct material thickness applies (typically carbon steel of a defined gauge or stainless).
- **Continuous upward slope.** The duct must slope continuously up toward the fan, commonly at least about 2 percent (around 20 mm per metre) on shorter horizontal runs and more on long runs, so liquefied grease drains back toward the hood and never pools.
- **Clearance to combustibles.** The duct must keep a listed clearance from combustible construction, or use a listed enclosure or wrap system that allows reduced clearance. Maintain the clearance for the full length, including through ceilings and walls.
- **Access and cleanout panels** at every change of direction and at intervals along straight runs, so the entire interior can be reached and cleaned. Panels are grease-tight and listed.
- **A dedicated duct per hood.** Each hood discharges through its own duct to its own fan; grease exhaust is not combined into a common system with general ventilation.
- **No dampers of any kind, anywhere in a grease duct.** No volume dampers, no fire dampers, no backdraft dampers. The duct must run completely unobstructed so grease drains and the duct can be cleaned end to end.
- **Run independently to the exterior.** The grease duct goes from hood to outdoors as a continuous, dedicated route and discharges away from intakes, openings and walking surfaces.

## The upblast roof exhaust fan

Grease exhaust normally terminates in an **upblast** roof-mounted fan that throws the discharge upward and clear of the roof. The fan is grease-rated, hinged so it can be lifted for cleaning, and fitted with a grease containment drain so runoff does not stain or soak the roof. The fan motor sits outside the airstream. Locate the discharge well away from any outdoor air intake, operable window or rooftop walking path so grease and odour are not drawn back into the building.

## Make-up air

Every litre per second exhausted must be replaced. If it is not, the kitchen goes strongly negative — doors are hard to open, the hood loses capture, combustion appliances can backdraft, and air is pulled in uncontrolled through every crack. Provide a dedicated **make-up air** unit sized to replace the great majority of the exhaust, with the building kept slightly negative relative to the dining area so cooking odours do not migrate. Make-up air should be tempered: heated where winters are cold, and, in hot or humid climates, cooled and dehumidified so the kitchen stays workable. Interlock the make-up air fan with the exhaust fan so they start and stop together.

## Wet chemical fire suppression

A Type I hood is protected by a **wet-chemical fire suppression system** with nozzles aimed at the appliances, the plenum and the duct collar. On activation — automatic via fusible links over the cooking line, or manual via a pull station — the system must:

- discharge wet-chemical agent over the cooking surfaces and into the hood,
- **shut down fuel and power** to the cooking appliances,
- and the exhaust fan generally continues to run to pull flames and agent into the hood rather than into the room.

The suppression system is interlocked so cooking cannot continue after a discharge, and it is inspected and serviced on a regular schedule.

## Cleaning intervals

Grease build-up is the fuel for a hood fire, so the system is cleaned on a frequency matched to the cooking load: very heavy or solid-fuel cooking is cleaned monthly, moderate-volume kitchens roughly quarterly, and light-duty operations roughly twice a year. Cleaning covers the hood, filters, the full duct interior through the access panels, and the fan. Keep dated cleaning records — they are checked by inspectors and insurers.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Commercial kitchens in Accra run extremely hot, and a strongly negative kitchen pulls in already hot, humid outdoor air that makes conditions worse for staff. Specify a tempered make-up air unit with cooling and dehumidification rather than a plain supply fan, and size exhaust generously for charcoal and high-heat local cooking. Enforcement of grease duct and suppression rules is uneven across districts, so hold the design to NFPA 96 regardless of what a local inspector requires — the fire risk is the same.

Match hood type to the appliances, size capture properly, build the grease duct welded and unobstructed, fit an upblast fan and tempered make-up air, and protect the line with interlocked wet-chemical suppression.
