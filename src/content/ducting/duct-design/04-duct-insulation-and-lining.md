Bare sheet metal is a poor barrier to heat and a good transmitter of fan and airflow noise. Duct insulation and acoustic lining solve both problems, but they are different products doing different jobs and are easy to confuse on site.

## Why insulate ductwork

A duct carrying conditioned air through an unconditioned space — a ceiling void, a riser, a plant room — exchanges heat with its surroundings through the thin metal wall. There are two consequences.

The first is **wasted energy**. Cold supply air gains heat on its way to the room, so the diffuser delivers air warmer than the design temperature and the coil has to work harder. A heating duct loses heat the same way. The second is **condensation**, which is dealt with separately below because it is the more dangerous failure.

Insulation reduces the heat flow by adding thermal resistance. The effectiveness is expressed as an R-value (m²·K/W in SI, or ft²·°F·h/Btu in IP). The higher the R-value, the slower the heat flow. Energy codes set minimum R-values that depend on where the duct runs and what it carries.

## External wrap versus internal liner

Insulation is applied in one of two positions, and the choice affects performance.

### External wrap

External wrap is a fibreglass or mineral-wool blanket with a factory facing, strapped or pinned to the outside of the duct. It is the most common arrangement. The full internal cross-section stays as clean bare metal, so the airway is smooth, easy to clean and gives a known friction loss.

### Internal liner

Internal liner is fixed to the inside of the duct. It insulates and absorbs noise in one product. The penalties are real: the liner occupies airway, so the duct must be sized larger to keep the same free area; the lined surface is rougher, raising friction; and the exposed face must be a coated, erosion-resistant grade so fibres are not shed into the airstream.

| Property | External wrap | Internal liner |
|---|---|---|
| Thermal job | yes | yes |
| Acoustic job | no | yes |
| Airway friction | unchanged | increased |
| Effective duct size | unchanged | reduced |
| Cleanability | easy | harder |

## Condensation control on cold ducts

This is the most important reason to insulate a chilled-air or chilled-water duct correctly.

The supply duct surface sits near the air temperature inside it — often 12 to 14 °C (54 to 57 °F). If the surrounding air is humid, its **dew point** can be higher than that surface temperature. Water vapour then condenses on the cold metal, exactly as it does on a cold drink. The condensate drips onto ceilings, stains finishes, corrodes the duct and feeds mould.

Insulation alone does not stop this. The insulation slows heat flow, but warm humid air can still migrate *through* a permeable blanket, reach the cold metal underneath and condense there — hidden from view. The defence is a **continuous vapour barrier** on the warm, outer face of the insulation: a foil or vinyl facing whose every seam, joint, staple line and penetration is sealed. The barrier must be unbroken. A single un-taped seam is a path for vapour, and condensation will form behind the insulation where no one sees it until the ceiling is wet.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Accra's hot, humid air carries a high dew point for most of the year, often above the surface temperature of a chilled-air duct. That makes condensation on cold ductwork a real and routine risk, not a rare one. Insist on a continuous vapour barrier with every seam, joint and hanger penetration sealed, and inspect it before the ceiling is closed — a missed seam will sweat and drip for the life of the building.

## Acoustic lining to cut noise

Fans, dampers and high-velocity airflow all generate noise that travels along the duct to the room. Acoustic lining is a sound-absorbing material — usually a coated fibrous blanket — fixed inside the duct to absorb that energy before it reaches the diffuser.

Lining is most effective on mid and high frequencies and is typically applied to the first few metres of supply and return duct nearest the air handler, around fans, and in cross-talk paths between rooms. It rarely needs to run the whole system. Because the lined face is also a thermal insulator, an internally lined duct often needs no separate external wrap on those sections — one product, two jobs. The trade-offs of liner noted above still apply: size the duct up to recover the lost free area.

## Typical thicknesses

Thickness follows the duty and the local energy code, but common starting points are useful.

| Application | Typical thickness mm | Typical thickness in |
|---|---|---|
| Supply duct in conditioned space | 25 | 1 |
| Supply duct in unconditioned void | 38 to 50 | 1.5 to 2 |
| Chilled-air duct, humid climate | 50 | 2 |
| Acoustic liner near air handler | 25 to 50 | 1 to 2 |

Two points carry the day on every job. Cold ducts need their vapour barrier continuous and sealed — that is non-negotiable. And insulation must be neatly fitted with no gaps at hangers, joints and fittings, because a small bare patch is where heat gain and condensation concentrate. Specify the R-value, specify a sealed vapour barrier on cold work, and inspect the install before the ceiling closes.
