A duct only performs as designed if it is built to withstand the pressure it carries without bulging, leaking or rattling. Construction quality is governed by the operating pressure class, the duct shape and the standards the specification names — most commonly SMACNA.

## Pressure classes drive everything

Before any gauge or joint is selected, the designer assigns a **pressure class** to each section of ductwork. The class is the maximum static pressure, positive or negative, that the duct will see in service. Common classes in inches of water gauge are 1/2, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6 and 10 in. w.g., which correspond roughly to 125, 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1500 and 2500 Pa.

Supply ductwork downstream of a variable air volume box is usually low pressure (250 Pa or under). Main supply trunks from the air handler before the boxes are medium pressure (500 to 750 Pa, that is 2 to 3 in. w.g.). Return and exhaust ducts are typically classed at 1 in. w.g. (250 Pa). A duct rated for 500 Pa fitted into a 1000 Pa run will deflect, drum and leak — so the class must be marked on the drawings section by section, not assumed.

## Galvanised steel gauge

The wall thickness of galvanised steel duct is selected from the pressure class and the longest side of the duct. Higher pressure and wider ducts need thicker metal and closer reinforcement. The table below shows a representative pattern for rectangular duct at 500 Pa (2 in. w.g.); always confirm against the current SMACNA construction tables for the actual class.

| Longest side mm | Approx in | Typical gauge | Sheet thickness mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| up to 300 | 12 | 26 ga | 0.55 |
| 350 to 750 | 14 to 30 | 24 ga | 0.70 |
| 800 to 1350 | 32 to 54 | 22 ga | 0.85 |
| 1400 to 2100 | 56 to 84 | 20 ga | 1.00 |
| over 2100 | over 84 | 18 ga | 1.31 |

Round and spiral duct can use lighter gauges for the same duty because the circular section resists pressure far better than a flat panel — there is no large flat face to bow.

## Rectangular round and spiral

The choice of shape is a trade-off between fit, cost and performance.

### Rectangular

Rectangular duct fits tight ceiling voids and is easy to transition. Its weakness is the flat side panels, which must be cross-broken or beaded and reinforced to stop them drumming under pressure. It also has more surface area per unit airflow, so it leaks and loses heat more readily than round.

### Round and spiral

Round duct is inherently rigid, uses less metal and seals better at the joints. **Spiral** duct is made from a continuous strip lock-formed into a helical seam — strong, fast to install and increasingly used on exposed architectural ceilings. Its limitation is depth: a deep void may not accept a large-diameter round duct, which is where flat-oval becomes a useful compromise.

## Joints seams and reinforcement

A duct is assembled from three connection types and each must suit the pressure class.

- **Longitudinal seams** run along the length of the duct. Pittsburgh lock and snap-lock seams are common; both are mechanically formed and, once sealed, are airtight.
- **Transverse joints** connect duct lengths end to end. Drive-slip and S-cleat joints serve low pressure; for medium and high pressure a flanged system such as a TDC or TDF integral flange, or a bolted companion-angle flange, is used.
- **Reinforcement** — angle iron, hat-section or tie rods — is added at intervals along flat panels of larger rectangular duct to control deflection. The hotter the pressure class, the closer the spacing.

The SMACNA construction standard pairs each pressure class with an allowable joint and reinforcement schedule. Following that schedule is what makes the duct legal against the specification.

## Sealing classes

Even a well-built duct leaks at its seams and joints unless it is deliberately sealed. SMACNA defines three seal classes:

| Seal class | What is sealed | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| A | All transverse joints, longitudinal seams and wall penetrations | 750 Pa and above, all supply |
| B | Transverse joints and longitudinal seams only | Medium pressure return |
| C | Transverse joints only | Low pressure 250 Pa and under |

Modern energy codes increasingly call for **Seal Class A on everything**, because air lost through a seam is fan energy paid for and thrown away. Sealants are water-based mastic, UL-listed tape or factory gaskets on flanged systems. Apply mastic to clean, dry metal and brush it well into the seam — a thin smear bridging a gap will crack and fail. Gasketed flange systems are fast but the gasket must be continuous and the flange bolted to the specified torque.

## Hangers and support spacing

Ductwork must be hung so it cannot sag, twist or transmit load to equipment. Support is by strap hangers or trapeze hangers from rod, fixed to the structure — never to other trades' pipework or ceiling grid.

A practical rule of thumb for rectangular galvanised duct is a support pair every **2.4 to 3.0 m (8 to 10 ft)**. Round duct can often run to 3.6 m (12 ft) between supports. Heavy items — fire dampers, large transitions, in-line fans — need dedicated hangers each side so their weight is not carried by the duct seam. Strap width and rod diameter scale with duct size; a 600 mm duct on 25 mm strap is typical, while a 1500 mm duct needs trapeze angle on threaded rod.

Hangers should be snug, not crushing the duct, and spaced so the bottom of a horizontal run stays visibly flat under its own weight and any insulation.

## The role of SMACNA in the spec

SMACNA — the Sheet Metal and Air Conditioning Contractors' National Association — publishes the *HVAC Duct Construction Standards* that the industry treats as the default. When a specification states "duct shall be constructed to SMACNA," it is importing the entire matched set of gauge, joint, reinforcement, sealing and support tables for the stated pressure class.

This matters for three reasons. It gives the contractor a buildable, costable target instead of a vague instruction. It gives the consultant an objective basis to reject sub-standard work. And it ensures the duct can actually hold its design pressure, so the leakage test it later faces is one it can pass. Specify the pressure class clearly, name SMACNA, and the construction details follow.
