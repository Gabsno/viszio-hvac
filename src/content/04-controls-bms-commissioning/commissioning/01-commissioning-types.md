Commissioning is the quality assurance process that verifies a building's systems actually perform the way the owner asked for and the designer intended. It is not a final inspection, and it is not a one-off event. Several distinct forms of commissioning exist, and a working HVAC engineer should know which one applies to a given building and stage of life.

## What commissioning is

Commissioning, usually written Cx, is a structured process that confirms HVAC, controls and related systems meet the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR). The OPR is the written statement of what the owner wants: comfort conditions, energy targets, redundancy, indoor air quality, hours of operation. Everything downstream is checked against it.

The crucial mindset shift is that commissioning verifies performance, not just installation. A chiller can be installed perfectly and still fail commissioning if it cannot hold leaving water temperature, or if its sequence wastes energy. Commissioning catches the gap between "installed" and "working as intended".

## New building commissioning

For a new building, commissioning runs in parallel with the whole project rather than at the end.

| Phase | Commissioning activity |
|---|---|
| Design | Review OPR and basis of design, check design against OPR |
| Construction | Review submittals, witness installation, run start-up checklists |
| Acceptance | Functional performance testing, witness every operating mode |
| Occupancy | Seasonal testing, training, near-warranty-end review |

Starting early is what makes commissioning effective. A design-phase review that finds a missing economizer or an unworkable sequence costs almost nothing to fix on paper. The same fault found after handover may need new ductwork. Construction-phase checklists confirm that equipment is installed, piped, wired and started correctly before any functional testing begins.

## Retro commissioning and recommissioning

Retro-commissioning applies the commissioning process to an existing building that was never formally commissioned. Many older buildings fall into this category. A retro-commissioning study investigates how the building actually runs, finds the gap against current needs, and produces a prioritized list of low-cost and no-cost improvements: corrected setpoints, repaired dampers, fixed schedules, retuned control loops.

Recommissioning is doing it again later for a building that was commissioned originally. Buildings drift. Sensors fall out of calibration, operators override sequences and forget to release them, and tenants change. Recommissioning every three to five years pulls the building back to intended performance.

Continuous commissioning, sometimes called monitoring-based commissioning, never stops. It uses live BMS data and fault detection analytics to flag faults as they appear, such as simultaneous heating and cooling, stuck dampers or schedules that no longer match occupancy. Instead of a periodic snapshot, the building is held at intended performance permanently.

## The commissioning authority

The commissioning authority, or CxA, is the person or firm leading the process. The CxA should be independent of the design and installation teams so that verification is not marking its own homework. The CxA writes the commissioning plan, develops the functional test scripts, witnesses testing, logs issues and verifies that each issue is resolved before sign-off. On a well-run project the CxA reports to the owner, which protects the owner's interests through construction and into operation.

## Typical findings and payback

Across building types, commissioning consistently uncovers the same families of fault: control sequences that were programmed differently from the specification, schedules that run equipment when the building is empty, economizers that never open, valves and dampers that leak, sensors reading several degrees off, and pumps or fans running at full speed when variable-speed control was intended.

These faults waste energy quietly. Studies of existing-building commissioning routinely report whole-building energy savings in the range of 5 to 15 percent, with simple payback periods often under two years and frequently under one. New-building commissioning also reduces warranty call-backs and handover disputes, because problems are found and fixed before the owner takes occupancy.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> In Accra's hot and humid climate, an HVAC system that drifts out of tune punishes the owner quickly, because cooling and dehumidification run hard for most of the year. Commissioning and periodic recommissioning are among the cheapest ways for a Ghanaian building owner to cut an electricity bill that is exposed to tariff increases and load shedding. Buildings pursuing IFC EDGE certification benefit especially, since EDGE energy savings only materialise if the installed systems are verified to behave as designed.
