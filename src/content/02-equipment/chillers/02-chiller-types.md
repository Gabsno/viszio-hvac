Chillers are usually classified by the compressor that drives the vapor compression cycle, or, in the case of absorption machines, by the fact that they use heat instead of a compressor at all. Each type occupies a distinct capacity band and has a characteristic part load signature that should drive selection.

## Scroll chillers

Scroll compressors use two interleaved spiral elements, one fixed and one orbiting, that progressively trap and compress refrigerant pockets toward the centre. They are simple, quiet, tolerant of liquid slugging and have few moving parts.

Individual scroll compressors are small, so scroll chillers are built for low tonnage, typically from around 15 to 500 kW, by ganging several compressors across one or more refrigerant circuits.

### Staging for part load

Because capacity is delivered in discrete compressor steps, a scroll chiller modulates by staging compressors on and off. A four compressor machine gives four crude capacity steps. Part load efficiency is therefore stepped rather than smooth, though multiple circuits help, and adding electronic expansion valves and variable speed on the lead compressor improves it considerably. Scroll chillers are common on packaged air cooled units serving small commercial buildings.

## Screw chillers

Screw compressors use one or two helical rotors that mesh to compress refrigerant continuously along the rotor length. They are robust, handle high pressure ratios well and suit medium to large duties, broadly 200 kW to 1.5 MW and beyond.

### Part load behaviour

Screw chillers modulate capacity with a slide valve that varies the effective rotor length, or with variable speed drives on the motor. This gives smooth, continuous capacity control rather than steps. Variable speed screw chillers post strong integrated part load values because reducing speed cuts compressor work faster than it cuts capacity. Screws are a workhorse choice for mid sized water cooled and air cooled plants.

## Centrifugal chillers

Centrifugal compressors are dynamic machines. A high speed impeller adds kinetic energy to the refrigerant, which a diffuser then converts to pressure. They achieve large flow with relatively modest lift, which makes them ideal for large tonnage, typically 700 kW to several MW in a single machine.

### Oil free magnetic bearing variants

Traditional centrifugals need an oil lubrication system for their bearings. Oil free machines levitate the shaft on magnetic bearings, removing oil from the refrigerant circuit. Oil fouling on heat exchanger surfaces is eliminated, friction losses fall and these machines deliver outstanding part load efficiency, especially with variable speed drives.

### Surge

Centrifugal compressors are prone to surge, an unstable flow reversal that occurs when the machine is asked for too much lift at too little flow, often at low load with a high condenser temperature. Surge causes noise, vibration and bearing wear. Modern controls use inlet guide vanes, variable speed and condenser relief to keep the operating point clear of the surge line.

## Absorption chillers

Absorption chillers replace the mechanical compressor with a thermochemical cycle driven by heat. The common type uses water as refrigerant and lithium bromide as absorbent. Low pressure water evaporates to produce cooling, the lithium bromide solution absorbs that vapour, and a heat source, steam, hot water or a gas burner, regenerates the solution.

Their electrical demand is very low because only small solution pumps run. They suit sites with waste heat, combined heat and power, or where electricity is costly or supply constrained, and where the cooling can offset an electrical peak. Their drawbacks are lower thermal efficiency, larger footprint, higher first cost and slower response.

## Capacity ranges compared

| Type | Typical capacity | Compressor | Part load control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scroll | 15 to 500 kW | Positive displacement | Compressor staging |
| Screw | 200 kW to 1.5 MW | Positive displacement | Slide valve or VSD |
| Centrifugal | 700 kW to several MW | Dynamic | Guide vanes and VSD |
| Absorption | 100 kW to several MW | None, heat driven | Heat input modulation |

## Why IPLV matters

Chillers rarely run at full load. Building load follows weather and occupancy, so a machine selected for a peak design day spends most hours between 40 and 80 percent capacity. Integrated part load value weights efficiency at 100, 75, 50 and 25 percent load to give a single number that better reflects seasonal energy use.

Two chillers with identical full load ratings can differ sharply in IPLV. Variable speed screw and centrifugal machines benefit from two effects at part load. Compressor speed falls, and condenser temperature drops because the heat rejection equipment is now oversized for the reduced load. Both reduce lift and compressor work. Selecting on full load rating alone hides this and can lead to a machine that looks efficient on paper but costs more to run.

## Selection guidance

Match the type to the load band first. Scroll for small packaged duties where simplicity and low first cost matter. Screw for medium to large loads needing smooth modulation and good part load performance. Centrifugal, preferably oil free magnetic bearing with variable speed, for large central plants where IPLV drives the energy bill. Absorption where heat is cheap or abundant and electrical demand must be limited.

For larger plants, consider multiple smaller chillers rather than one large machine. Staging two or three units lets the plant keep each running near its efficient band, improves redundancy and avoids forcing a single large chiller down to a load where it surges or cycles. Always compare candidates on IPLV and on part load performance at the site's actual condenser conditions, not on full load rating alone.
