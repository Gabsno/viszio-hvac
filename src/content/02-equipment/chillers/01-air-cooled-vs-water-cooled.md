Choosing between an air cooled and a water cooled chiller is one of the earliest and most consequential decisions in a central plant design. The two machines run the same vapor compression cycle but reject condenser heat in fundamentally different ways, and that single difference drives efficiency, plant room scope, footprint, water use and life cycle cost.

## How each rejects heat

Every chiller must dump the evaporator load plus the compressor work to a heat sink. The question is what that sink is and at what temperature the refrigerant condenses against it.

An air cooled chiller rejects heat directly to ambient dry bulb air through a finned condenser coil and propeller fans, usually as a single packaged unit sited outdoors. On a hot day the condensing temperature must sit roughly 10 to 15 °C above ambient dry bulb, so a 35 °C design day forces condensing near 48 to 55 °C.

A water cooled chiller rejects heat to condenser water, which is in turn cooled by a cooling tower that evaporates a small fraction of that water. Because evaporative cooling chases the ambient wet bulb rather than the dry bulb, condenser water can be delivered at around 29 to 32 °C even on a hot day, letting the refrigerant condense near 35 to 40 °C.

## Why water cooled is more efficient

Compressor work scales with the lift, the gap between evaporating and condensing pressure. Lowering the condensing temperature shrinks that lift directly. A water cooled machine condensing 12 to 15 °C cooler than its air cooled equivalent therefore draws materially less power for the same cooling output.

Rated full load efficiency reflects this clearly. The table below gives typical figures rated per AHRI 550/590, expressed as coefficient of performance and as kW per kW of cooling.

| Parameter | Air cooled | Water cooled |
|---|---|---|
| Full load COP | 2.8 to 3.4 | 5.0 to 6.5 |
| Full load kW per kW | 0.30 to 0.36 | 0.15 to 0.20 |
| Typical IPLV COP | 3.8 to 5.0 | 6.5 to 9.0 |
| Condensing temperature | High, tracks dry bulb | Low, tracks wet bulb |

Integrated part load value matters because chillers spend most operating hours below full load. Water cooled machines, especially centrifugal types, post excellent IPLV figures as condenser water relief and compressor speed both improve at part load.

## The extra scope of water cooled

Lower energy is not free. A water cooled plant carries a chain of supporting systems that an air cooled plant does not need.

### Cooling tower and condenser loop

A water cooled chiller needs a cooling tower, condenser water pumps, interconnecting pipework, valves and controls. The tower must be sited with clear air paths and protected against recirculation of its own warm plume.

### Water treatment and makeup

Evaporation concentrates dissolved solids, so the loop needs chemical dosing, a controlled bleed and a reliable makeup water supply. Without treatment, scale, corrosion and biological fouling degrade the condenser approach and erode the very efficiency that justified the choice.

### Plant room and operator attention

Water cooled plant is best housed in a dedicated plant room and benefits from a competent operator. Air cooled plant is largely self contained and tolerates lighter supervision.

## First cost versus life cycle cost

Air cooled plant usually wins on first cost. There is no tower, no condenser pumps, no condenser pipework and far less commissioning. Water cooled plant costs more to install but recovers that premium through lower energy bills.

A simple life cycle comparison should weigh capital, energy over the analysis period, water and treatment cost, and maintenance. For large loads with long annual run hours and reasonable electricity tariffs, water cooled plant typically returns its premium within three to six years. For small loads or short run hours, the payback stretches and air cooled often wins outright.

## Footprint siting and water availability

Air cooled chillers need significant outdoor space with unobstructed airflow, commonly at roof level, and they reject heat and noise to that location. Water cooled chillers are compact in the plant room but the tower still needs an exposed, well ventilated position.

Water availability is often the deciding constraint. A water cooled plant consumes makeup water continuously. Where water is scarce, expensive or unreliable, that recurring demand can rule the technology out regardless of the energy case.

## When air cooled wins

Air cooled chillers are the sensible default for small to medium loads, for sites with no water or unreliable supply, where simplicity and minimal operator skill are priorities, and where first cost dominates the decision. They are also attractive where freeze risk, plume nuisance or Legionella management would otherwise add cost and risk.

Water cooled chillers win for large central plants with long run hours, where energy cost is significant, where a plant room and skilled operators exist, and where treated makeup water is dependable.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Accra's high wet bulb, often near 26 to 27 °C, narrows the gap that makes water cooled plant attractive, since the tower cannot deliver condenser water as cold as in a drier climate. Hard municipal water and intermittent supply make robust treatment and storage essential, and unreliable grid power means cooling tower fans and condenser pumps add to the generator and changeover load. For many mid sized Accra buildings, air cooled chillers remain the pragmatic choice; water cooled plant pays off mainly on large, well run installations with secure water and power.

## Selection summary

Start from load size, annual operating hours, water availability, electricity tariff and the level of operator skill on site. Where most of those favour energy savings and the supporting infrastructure exists, choose water cooled. Where water or skill is scarce and loads are modest, choose air cooled and accept the higher condensing temperature as the price of simplicity.
