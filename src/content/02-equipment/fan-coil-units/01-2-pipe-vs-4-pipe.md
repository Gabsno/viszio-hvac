A fan coil unit, or FCU, is a small terminal device that conditions the air in a single zone using a fan and a water-fed coil. Whether a building's FCUs are piped as a 2-pipe or a 4-pipe system is a decision that fixes what each zone can do, and it should be made deliberately at design stage.

## What a fan coil unit is

An FCU is a compact box containing a fan, one or two finned-tube coils, a filter and a drain pan. It is fed with chilled water or hot water from a central plant and recirculates the air within its zone across the coil. FCUs come in many forms — ceiling-concealed ducted, exposed cassette, wall-mounted and floor-standing — and a typical unit serves one room or a small open area.

An important limitation defines how FCUs are used: most units handle only recirculated room air and bring in little or no outdoor air of their own. Ventilation is therefore not their job. Fresh air must be supplied separately, usually by a dedicated outdoor air system that pre-conditions ventilation air and delivers it to the space or directly into each FCU.

## Two pipe systems

A 2-pipe FCU has a single coil served by one supply pipe and one return pipe. The plant pushes either chilled water or hot water through that common pair of pipes, so the entire building operates in one mode at a time. Switching the building from cooling to heating means a seasonal changeover at the plant — the chillers are stopped and a boiler or heat source is started, and the water in the loop changes from cold to hot.

The consequence is that no zone can have heating while another has cooling. During mild shoulder seasons, when some rooms want warmth and others want cooling, a 2-pipe system can only serve one demand. Its appeal is cost and simplicity: half the pipework, fewer valves and a smaller installation.

## Four pipe systems

A 4-pipe FCU has two separate coils — or a single coil with isolated circuits — one fed by a chilled-water supply and return pair, the other by a hot-water supply and return pair. Each zone's controls draw on whichever circuit it needs, so any room can be in heating while any other is in cooling, at any time of year, with no plant changeover.

This flexibility costs more: twice the pipework, more valves and controls, and a plant that produces hot and cold water simultaneously.

## Comparing the two arrangements

| Aspect | 2 pipe | 4 pipe |
|---|---|---|
| Pipes per FCU | One supply one return | Two supply two return |
| Coils | One shared coil | Separate heating and cooling |
| Simultaneous modes | No | Yes |
| Changeover | Seasonal at plant | None needed |
| Installed cost | Lower | Higher |
| Best fit | Cooling-dominated buildings | Mixed-demand buildings |

## Choosing between them

The right choice follows the building's load pattern. A 4-pipe system is justified where zones genuinely have opposing demands at the same time — buildings with significant perimeter heating loads, or facilities mixing heat-generating interior rooms with façade-exposed offices.

In a cooling-dominated tropical climate, heating demand is rare or absent, so the simultaneous capability of a 4-pipe system would sit unused. The 2-pipe system is the common and sensible choice there: it delivers chilled water all year, costs far less to install and avoids the complexity of a hot-water plant that is seldom needed.

## Condensate management

Because an FCU cooling coil runs below the air dewpoint, it condenses water continuously. Every unit needs a sloped, watertight drain pan piped to a drain, and concealed units frequently need a small condensate pump where gravity drainage is not available. In humid climates the condensate volume is significant, and overflowing or blocked drains are a leading cause of ceiling damage. Drain pans, traps and pipe falls deserve careful attention on every FCU.

## Control valves and thermostats

Each FCU coil is regulated by a control valve driven from a room thermostat. The valve may be a simple two-position type, opening and closing the coil, or a modulating type that throttles flow proportionally for tighter temperature control. The fan itself may run at fixed speeds selected by the thermostat or at variable speed. On a 4-pipe unit, the controls coordinate the heating and cooling valves so the two coils are never working against each other, which would waste energy heating and cooling the same air.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Accra's climate is almost entirely cooling-driven with ambient design near 33°C, so 2-pipe chilled-water FCU systems are the practical default and the extra cost of 4-pipe is rarely justified. High humidity means heavy, continuous condensate, so size drain pans and pumps generously and keep drains accessible for cleaning. Pair FCUs with a dedicated outdoor air system so ventilation is met independently, since the units bring in little fresh air themselves; most FCUs and valves are imported, so confirm coil materials suit the coastal environment.
