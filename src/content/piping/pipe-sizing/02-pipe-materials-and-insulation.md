The pipe carries the water; the insulation protects the energy in it and the building around it. Choosing the right material for each service and insulating it correctly are basic skills that decide whether a hydronic system is reliable and efficient.

## Common HVAC pipe materials

Three broad families cover most HVAC piping: steel, copper and plastics. Each has a service range where it makes sense.

| Material | Typical service | Strengths | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Black steel | Chilled water, hot water, condenser water mains | Strong, cheap in large sizes, high temperature | Corrodes, heavy, needs skilled jointing |
| Copper | Smaller hot and chilled branches, refrigerant | Corrosion resistant, neat, easy to joint | Costly in large sizes |
| PPR | Chilled and moderate hot water | Corrosion proof, light, smooth bore | Limited temperature, larger wall, support spacing |
| HDPE | Buried condenser and make-up lines | Tough, corrosion proof, flexible | Heat fusion needed, low temperature only |

**Black steel** is the traditional choice for closed loop mains and risers, especially at larger diameters where its strength and lower cost win. It tolerates high temperatures comfortably. Its weaknesses are weight, the skill needed to weld or thread it, and corrosion if oxygen is present, which is why closed loops are de-aerated and treated.

**Copper** is corrosion resistant, neat and quick to joint, and it suits smaller hot and chilled water branches and is standard for refrigerant pipework. Its cost climbs steeply with diameter, so it is rarely used for large mains.

**Plastics** have grown in HVAC use. **PPR**, polypropylene random copolymer, is corrosion proof, light and has a smooth low-friction bore, suiting chilled water and moderate temperature hot water. Its pressure rating falls as temperature rises and it needs closer pipe supports than steel because it is less stiff. **HDPE**, high density polyethylene, is tough and flexible, well suited to buried condenser water and make-up lines where its corrosion immunity and resistance to ground movement are valuable.

## Matching material to service and temperature

The selection logic is straightforward once the service is known. The deciding factors are operating temperature, operating pressure, diameter and whether the line is buried or exposed.

Hot water and steam push toward steel because plastics lose strength as they warm; a PPR system rated comfortably for 7 °C chilled water may be marginal at 80 °C heating. Large mains favour steel for cost and rigidity. Small branches and equipment connections favour copper or plastic for ease of installation. Buried lines favour HDPE for corrosion immunity and joint integrity underground. Always confirm the manufacturer's pressure-temperature rating for plastics, because the headline pressure rating assumes a reference temperature and derates above it.

## Jointing methods

How pipe is joined affects labour, leak risk and future maintenance.

- **Welding** suits steel mains, giving the strongest, most permanent joint, but it needs a qualified welder and hot work controls.
- **Threading** suits smaller steel pipe, simple but slower and a potential leak path if not sealed properly.
- **Grooved couplings** clamp over rolled or cut grooves in steel pipe, fast to install, allow slight movement and are easy to dismantle for service.
- **Soldering or brazing** joins copper, with brazing giving the higher strength needed for refrigerant lines.
- **Press fittings** crimp onto copper or steel with a tool, fast and flame free.
- **Heat fusion** joins PPR and HDPE by melting and pressing the surfaces together, producing a homogeneous joint as strong as the pipe.

## Pipe insulation for chilled and hot lines

Insulation does two jobs. On hot pipe it cuts heat loss, saving energy and protecting people from burns. On chilled pipe it cuts heat gain, but more importantly it keeps the pipe surface above the dew point so moisture does not condense on it.

Common insulation materials are closed-cell elastomeric foam, popular on chilled lines for its built-in moisture resistance; mineral wool, favoured on hot pipe for higher temperature tolerance; and rigid phenolic or polyisocyanurate sections. Thickness is chosen from the pipe size, the operating temperature and the surrounding conditions, with chilled lines in humid spaces needing more thickness than the same pipe in a dry plant room.

### The vapour barrier on cold pipe

A chilled water pipe runs colder than the surrounding air. Warm humid air will always try to drive moisture inward toward that cold surface. If water vapour reaches the cold pipe, it condenses inside the insulation, soaks it, destroys its thermal performance and corrodes the pipe.

The defence is a continuous **vapour barrier** on the warm, outer face of cold-line insulation. It must be unbroken: sealed at every joint, every fitting, every valve and every support. A single gap lets moisture migrate in and condense, and the damage spreads invisibly under the cladding. Closed-cell elastomeric foam is itself a vapour retarder, which is why its joints are glued, not just taped. Hot pipe does not need a vapour barrier, because the pipe is warmer than the air and moisture is driven outward, away from the surface.

### Condensation control

Condensation control is about keeping every cold surface above the dew point of the air around it. That means insulating not just straight pipe but valves, flanges, strainers, pump bodies and pipe supports, all of which become cold bridges if left bare. Pipe supports should use insulated saddles or load-bearing inserts so the clamp does not pierce the vapour barrier and create a sweating point.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Accra's hot, humid air has a high dew point for much of the year, so any bare or poorly sealed cold surface will sweat. Chilled water pipework above ceilings must be insulated with a fully intact vapour barrier, with every joint, fitting and support sealed, or condensation will drip, stain ceiling tiles and corrode pipe. Specifying closed-cell elastomeric insulation with glued seams and insulated pipe supports is the reliable local practice, and it should be inspected before ceilings are closed up.
