Direct expansion (DX) cooling is the most common form of air conditioning on the planet, and for good reason: it is simple, self-contained, and removes one whole fluid loop from the design. This article explains what DX means, the main system formats, and where each one earns its place.

## What direct expansion means

In a DX system the refrigerant evaporates directly inside the coil that the conditioned air passes over. There is no intermediate water loop. The evaporator coil is the cooling coil, the refrigerant boils inside it at roughly 5 to 7 °C, and the air giving up heat to that boiling refrigerant leaves the coil cold.

Contrast this with a chilled water system, where the chiller cools water, the water is pumped to air handlers, and a separate water coil cools the air. DX skips the middle step. Fewer components, fewer pumps, less commissioning, and a lower first cost on small projects. The price you pay is refrigerant piping limits and a ceiling on how large a single system can practically grow.

Every DX system contains the same four components: a compressor, a condenser, an expansion device, and an evaporator. The differences between split, multi-split and packaged units are simply about which components sit in which box and how far apart those boxes can be.

## Split systems

A split system divides the refrigerant circuit into an indoor unit and an outdoor unit, joined by an insulated copper line set. The compressor and condenser live outside; the evaporator and supply fan live inside.

### Indoor unit formats

- **Wall-mounted (hi-wall):** the familiar small box high on a wall. Cheap, fast to install, ideal for single rooms up to roughly 7 kW.
- **Ceiling cassette:** recessed into a suspended ceiling, throwing air in two or four directions. Tidy and good for open offices and shops.
- **Ducted (concealed):** a slim air handler hidden above the ceiling, feeding short duct runs and several diffusers. The right choice when you want a clean ceiling with no visible equipment.

### Multi-split systems

A multi-split connects several indoor units to one outdoor unit, each indoor unit serving its own zone with its own thermostat. It reduces the number of condensers cluttering a facade. A multi-split is essentially a small-scale cousin of VRF; true VRF extends the idea with longer pipe runs, more indoor units, and simultaneous heating and cooling.

## Packaged and rooftop units

A packaged unit puts all four components into a single cabinet. A rooftop unit (RTU) is the classic example: compressor, condenser, evaporator and supply fan in one weatherproof box on the roof, connected to the building only by supply and return ductwork and power.

Packaged units shine on single-storey retail, warehouses, restaurants and light commercial buildings where roof space is free and duct runs are short. They are factory-charged and factory-tested, so site work is mostly rigging, ducting and electrical. They also accept add-ons like economizer dampers, gas heat and improved filtration without redesign.

| Format | Typical capacity | Best fit | Main limit |
|--------|------------------|----------|------------|
| Wall split | 2 to 7 kW | Single rooms, small offices | One zone only |
| Cassette split | 3.5 to 14 kW | Shops, open offices | Ceiling void needed |
| Ducted split | 5 to 28 kW | Apartments, small floors | Limited external static |
| Multi-split | 7 to 30 kW | Several rooms, one condenser | Pipe length limits |
| Packaged RTU | 7 to 250 kW | Retail, warehouse, restaurant | Needs roof and ductwork |

## Reading the efficiency ratings

DX equipment is rated to standards such as AHRI 210/240 for unitary air conditioners and heat pumps. Two numbers matter most.

- **EER (Energy Efficiency Ratio):** cooling output divided by electrical input at a single rated condition, expressed in W/W or Btu/h per watt. It tells you full-load efficiency on a design-hot day.
- **SEER (Seasonal EER):** a weighted average across a range of part-load conditions, reflecting how the unit performs over a cooling season.

SEER rewards equipment that modulates well at part load, which is most of the operating year. An inverter-driven compressor that ramps down to 30 percent capacity holds high efficiency at light load; a fixed-speed compressor that can only cycle on and off does not. A modern inverter split may reach SEER values well above a basic fixed-speed unit of the same rated capacity. When comparing quotations, confirm both units were rated to the same standard and test conditions, otherwise the numbers are not comparable.

## Choosing between DX, VRF and chilled water

DX is the default below roughly 30 to 50 kW of total cooling, or where zones are scattered and a central plant cannot be justified. Above that, the calculus shifts.

- **VRF** extends DX with long refrigerant piping, many indoor units, heat recovery between zones and excellent part-load efficiency. It suits medium offices and hotels where diversity is high.
- **Chilled water** wins on large buildings: water pipes carry cooling far more compactly than refrigerant lines, plant rooms centralise maintenance, and a few large chillers beat dozens of small condensers on lifecycle cost.

DX trade-offs to weigh: refrigerant line length and lift caps the geometry; many small condensers mean many filters and many service visits; and a refrigerant leak is harder to locate than a water leak. Against that, DX has the lowest first cost, the simplest commissioning, and partial redundancy because one failed unit does not take down the building.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> On most small and medium projects in Accra, wall and cassette splits dominate because they are cheap, available, and quick to install without a plant room. Specify inverter units rather than fixed-speed ones: they hold efficiency through the long hot-humid cooling season and cope better with the voltage swings common on the local grid. Pair each unit with a properly rated voltage stabiliser, since compressors fail early when fed under-voltage or surging supply, and imported spares can take weeks to arrive.

## Practical design notes

Size DX equipment to a real load calculation, not a rule of thumb. Oversized DX short-cycles, never runs long enough to dehumidify, and leaves rooms cold and clammy. Keep refrigerant line sets within the manufacturer's stated length and lift limits and add charge for long runs. Give condensers clear air on all sides so they do not recirculate their own hot discharge, and never bury an outdoor unit in an unventilated enclosure.
