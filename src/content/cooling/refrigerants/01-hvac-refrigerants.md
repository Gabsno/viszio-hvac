A refrigerant is the working fluid that carries heat out of a building. Picking one is no longer a free choice: safety classification, global warming potential and a shifting regulatory landscape all narrow the field. This article explains what a refrigerant must do, how ASHRAE 34 classifies them, and how the common fluids compare.

## What a refrigerant must do

A good refrigerant has to perform a specific thermodynamic job and behave well doing it.

- **Boil and condense at useful pressures.** It must evaporate near 0 to 7 °C without pulling a deep vacuum, and condense near 40 to 55 °C without demanding extreme pressure.
- **Carry a lot of heat per kilogram.** A high latent heat of vaporisation means smaller pipes, smaller compressors and lower mass flow.
- **Be chemically stable** inside the circuit, yet break down in the atmosphere if it escapes.
- **Be compatible** with copper, steel, compressor oil and elastomer seals.
- **Be safe and affordable**, with low toxicity and manageable flammability.

No single fluid is perfect at all of these, which is why the industry uses a family of refrigerants rather than one universal answer.

## The ASHRAE 34 safety classes

ASHRAE Standard 34 assigns every refrigerant a two-character safety group. The letter rates toxicity, the number rates flammability.

- **Toxicity:** Class A is lower toxicity; Class B is higher toxicity.
- **Flammability:** Class 1 is no flame propagation; Class 2L is mildly flammable with low burning velocity; Class 2 is flammable; Class 3 is higher flammability.

| Class | Toxicity | Flammability | Example |
|-------|----------|--------------|---------|
| A1 | Lower | None | R-410A, R-134a |
| A2L | Lower | Mild, low velocity | R-32, R-454B |
| A3 | Lower | Higher | R-290 |
| B1 | Higher | None | R-744 group varies |
| B2L | Higher | Mild | R-717 ammonia |

The A2L class is central to where the industry is heading. These fluids will burn, but only weakly and only at relatively high concentrations, so the risk is manageable with sensible charge limits, leak detection and ventilation. The standard pairs with ASHRAE 15 to set charge limits per room.

## The common refrigerants

### R-410A

For two decades the workhorse of split, multi-split and packaged DX equipment. It is A1, so non-flammable and easy to handle. Its drawback is a high GWP near 2088, which makes it a target for phase-down. New equipment is steadily moving away from it.

### R-32

A single-component A2L fluid with a GWP near 675, roughly a third of R-410A. It is efficient, uses slightly less charge, and has become the standard refrigerant in much of the global split-system market. Mild flammability requires attention to charge limits and brazing practice.

### R-134a

An A1 fluid used in chillers, automotive air conditioning and medium-temperature applications. Its GWP near 1430 means it is also being displaced, with R-513A and R-1234ze appearing as lower-GWP successors in chillers.

### R-454B

An A2L blend with a GWP near 466, positioned as a direct successor to R-410A in many new heat pumps and DX systems. It offers a meaningful GWP cut while keeping performance close to the fluid it replaces.

### R-290 propane

An A3 hydrocarbon with a GWP near 3, essentially negligible. It is highly efficient but flammable, so it is restricted to small-charge sealed systems such as monobloc heat pumps, display cabinets and small splits where the charge can be tightly limited.

### R-744 carbon dioxide

A natural refrigerant, A1, with a GWP of 1. It works at very high pressures and often runs transcritically, demanding purpose-built components. It dominates commercial refrigeration in cold climates and is growing in heat pump water heating.

### R-717 ammonia

A natural refrigerant, B2L, with a GWP of 0. Thermodynamically excellent and cheap, but toxic, so it is confined to industrial plant rooms with trained operators and strict detection, never to occupied-space equipment.

## GWP and ODP explained

Two environmental metrics drive refrigerant policy.

- **ODP (Ozone Depletion Potential)** measures damage to the ozone layer. The old CFCs and HCFCs such as R-22 had significant ODP and are now phased out under the Montreal Protocol. Today's HFCs and HFOs have zero ODP.
- **GWP (Global Warming Potential)** measures heat-trapping strength relative to carbon dioxide over 100 years. CO2 is defined as 1. An HFC with GWP 2000 means one kilogram leaked equals two tonnes of CO2.

The current pressure is entirely about GWP. The Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol commits countries to a stepped phase-down of high-GWP HFCs. The market response is a clear migration toward lower-GWP fluids, and because most low-GWP options are mildly flammable, A2L refrigerants are now the mainstream direction for new air conditioning equipment.

## How to choose

Match the refrigerant to the equipment, the charge size and the space it serves. For a small split, R-32 or R-454B is the modern default. For a chiller, expect R-1234ze, R-513A or R-514A. For a small sealed appliance, R-290 is excellent. For industrial process cooling, ammonia or CO2. Always check the room charge limit under ASHRAE 15 before committing, and never mix or top up a system with a refrigerant other than the one on its nameplate.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> The global shift to A2L blends arrives unevenly in Accra. R-410A and R-22 stock still circulates, and genuine R-32 or R-454B can be harder to source reliably, which tempts contractors toward unbranded or counterfeit cylinders. Equally important, A2L fluids demand technicians trained in their brazing and leak-detection practices, and that training base is still thin locally. Specify equipment whose refrigerant you can actually buy and service in-country, and budget for proper recovery rather than venting old charge.
