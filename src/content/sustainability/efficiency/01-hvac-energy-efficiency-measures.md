HVAC is typically the largest single energy user in a commercial building, often 40 to 60 percent of the total. The good news is that a large fraction of that energy is recoverable through a well understood menu of measures — some costing almost nothing, others requiring capital. This article walks through that menu.

## Start by right sizing

The single most influential efficiency decision is made before any equipment is bought: choosing the correct size. Engineers habitually oversize chillers, air handlers and fan coils to be "safe", but an oversized system runs at part load almost all the time, short cycles, controls humidity poorly and costs more to buy and to run.

A load calculation done honestly — with realistic internal gains, accurate envelope data and sensible safety margins of perhaps 10 to 15 percent rather than 50 — usually produces equipment one or two sizes smaller than rule of thumb. Right sizing improves efficiency for the entire life of the building and costs nothing extra; it is simply the discipline of trusting a proper calculation.

## Variable speed everything

Fans and pumps obey the **affinity laws**: power varies with the cube of speed. Slowing a fan to 80 percent of its speed cuts its power to roughly 50 percent. Because buildings spend most hours below peak load, variable speed drives (VSDs) on fans and pumps are among the most reliable savings available.

Replace constant volume fans with VAV systems, fit VSDs to chilled and condenser water pumps with proper differential pressure control, and use variable speed compressors where the equipment offers them. The cube law means even modest speed reductions, sustained across thousands of operating hours, dominate the annual energy bill.

## Efficient central plant

When a chiller is replaced, the efficiency gap between an old machine and a current one is large. Modern chillers report performance as:

- **COP** — coefficient of performance, cooling output divided by power input.
- **kW per ton** — power per ton of refrigeration; lower is better.
- **IPLV** — integrated part load value, a weighted average across part load points that reflects real operation.

A chiller may rate well at full load but poorly at part load, so the IPLV often matters more than the headline figure. Specify against both, and stage multiple chillers so each runs near its sweet spot rather than one large machine loafing.

## Free cooling and heat recovery

Two measures move energy around the building instead of generating it.

An **economizer** uses cool outdoor air directly for cooling when conditions allow, letting the chiller idle. In a temperate climate an air side economizer can deliver hundreds of free cooling hours a year.

**Heat recovery** captures energy that would be thrown away. An energy recovery ventilator transfers heat — and, with an enthalpy wheel, moisture — between exhaust and incoming fresh air, cutting the load of conditioning ventilation air. Condenser heat recovery reuses rejected heat for service water or reheat.

## Controls setpoints and ventilation

Some of the cheapest savings need only a competent controls technician and an afternoon.

| Measure | Cost | Typical effect |
|---------|------|----------------|
| Correct schedules | Very low | Plant off when unoccupied |
| Widen setpoint deadband | Very low | Less simultaneous heat and cool |
| Supply air and water reset | Low | Less compressor and reheat energy |
| Demand controlled ventilation | Low to medium | Fresh air follows occupancy |

Tightening schedules so plant stops when the building is empty, widening the deadband between heating and cooling setpoints to perhaps 3 to 4 K, and resetting supply air and chilled water temperatures upward at part load all cut energy with no comfort penalty. **Demand controlled ventilation** uses CO2 sensors to modulate outdoor air to actual occupancy rather than a fixed maximum, saving the energy of conditioning air nobody is breathing.

## Envelope shading and maintenance

The HVAC system only has to remove the load the building lets in. Reducing solar gain through external shading, solar control glazing, reflective roofing and added insulation shrinks the cooling load directly — and a smaller load lets the next equipment replacement be smaller too.

Routine maintenance protects every gain above. Clean coils, clean filters changed on schedule, correct refrigerant charge, sealed ductwork and calibrated sensors keep installed efficiency from drifting away. A fouled condenser coil alone can add 20 to 30 percent to compressor energy.

## Low cost versus capital measures

It helps to sort the menu by what it asks of the budget.

**Low and no cost measures** — schedules, setpoints, resets, demand controlled ventilation, sealing ducts, filter and coil maintenance — need mostly attention and a controls technician. They often pay back in months and should always be done first; there is no sense buying an efficient chiller to feed a building that runs all night.

**Capital measures** — replacing chillers, fitting variable speed drives, adding heat recovery, upgrading the envelope — need investment and are justified on simple payback or life cycle cost. The sound strategy is to capture the cheap savings immediately, then time the capital measures to coincide with equipment reaching end of life, when the marginal cost of choosing the efficient option is smallest. Energy standards such as ASHRAE Standard 90.1 set the minimum efficiency floor; a deliberate efficiency strategy is about how far, and how cost effectively, to climb above it.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> In Ghana, where electricity tariffs are high and the grid is unreliable, efficiency is not only an environmental choice but a direct financial one. Every kilowatt the HVAC system does not draw is a kilowatt that does not have to be supplied by a diesel generator during an outage, so right sizing and efficiency measures cut both the utility bill and generator fuel. The Ghana Energy Commission promotes efficient equipment and minimum standards, and for buildings that rely on standby generation, a smaller, well controlled HVAC system materially reduces running cost.
