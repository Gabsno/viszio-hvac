Electric resistance heating is the simplest way to make heat — pass a current through a resistance element and it gets hot. It is also one of the most misunderstood, because it is wonderfully efficient at the point of use yet often the most expensive choice to run.

## How electric resistance heating works

When current flows through a resistive element, electrical energy converts directly into heat. There is no combustion, no flue, no moving fuel, and almost no loss. Several forms appear in HVAC systems.

### Duct heaters

A duct heater is a bank of resistance elements mounted inside a duct. Air blown across the elements picks up heat. Duct heaters are common as **terminal reheat** in variable air volume systems, lifting the temperature of cooled supply air for a zone that needs warming. They need an airflow proven by a flow switch and a high-limit cutout, because elements with no air across them overheat quickly.

### Electric coils

An electric coil sits inside an air handling unit and serves the same role as a hot water coil — heating the air stream — but with elements instead of a finned water coil. It removes the need for a boiler, pipework, and pumps, which makes it attractive for small units or where running a wet system is impractical.

### Immersion heaters

An immersion heater is an element fitted directly into a hot water cylinder or tank. It heats the water in contact with it. Immersion heaters are the standard backup in domestic hot water cylinders and the primary heat source in small or simple installations.

## Efficient at the point of use

An electric heater is close to **100 percent efficient** at the point of use. Essentially every kilowatt-hour of electricity becomes a kilowatt-hour of heat in the space or the water. That headline figure is true and is why electric heating is often described as the most efficient form of heating.

The headline is also misleading, because it ignores two costs.

The first is **price**. A unit of electricity usually costs three to five times as much as the same energy delivered as gas. So even at 100 percent efficiency, an electric heater can cost far more to run than a gas boiler at 90 percent efficiency.

The second is **carbon**. Electricity is only as clean as the grid that makes it. Where generation is fossil heavy, the carbon behind a unit of electric heat can exceed the carbon of burning gas directly, because the power station throws away heat that a boiler keeps.

| Heat source | Point of use efficiency | Relative running cost | Carbon depends on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electric resistance | About 100 percent | High | Grid generation mix |
| Condensing gas boiler | 90 to 98 percent | Low to medium | Gas combustion |
| Heat pump | 250 to 450 percent effective | Low | Grid generation mix |

## Where electric heating fits

Electric resistance heating is rarely the right answer for heating a whole building, but it is the right answer for several specific duties.

- **Small reheat loads.** Where a single VAV zone or a small space needs a modest temperature lift, a compact duct heater avoids the cost of extending a wet heating system to one terminal.
- **Freeze protection.** Trace heating on pipework, drain lines, and cooling coils prevents freezing in cold conditions. The load is small and intermittent, so running cost barely matters and reliability is what counts.
- **Low first cost.** Electric heating has no boiler, no flue, no fuel store, and minimal pipework, so the capital cost and the installation footprint are low. For a small building, a tenant fit-out, or a temporary installation, that simplicity can outweigh the higher running cost.
- **Standby and backup.** An immersion heater backs up a primary heat source and covers it during maintenance or failure.

## Contrast with the heat pump

The honest comparison for electric heating is not against gas — it is against the heat pump, which is also an electric heater. A heat pump does not make heat; it moves heat from outside air, water, or ground into the building. For each unit of electricity it consumes it delivers three or four units of heat, because most of that heat is harvested rather than generated.

So a heat pump uses roughly a third of the electricity of a resistance heater for the same comfort, with a third of the running cost and a third of the carbon. Wherever heating runs for many hours — space heating, bulk water heating — the heat pump is the better electric choice and the running-cost saving repays its higher capital cost. Electric resistance heating wins only where the load is small, brief, or occasional, and the simplicity and low first cost matter more than the cost per kilowatt-hour.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Ghana is a hot climate where comfort space heating is rarely needed, so electric heating here mostly serves hot water, process duties, and dehumidification reheat. With Ghana grid tariffs on the high side and supply not always reliable, electric resistance heating is best confined to small reheat coils, freeze protection, and standby immersion duty rather than any bulk heating role. For real hot water demand, a heat pump water heater delivers the same hot water for roughly a third of the electricity, which is the saving that matters against local tariffs.

## Selecting and protecting electric heaters

When an electric heater is the right choice, size it to the calculated load with only a small margin, since oversizing wastes capacity and worsens cycling. Confirm the electrical supply can carry the load and that the distribution board has capacity. Always fit an **airflow or flow interlock** and a manual-reset **high-limit cutout** so an element can never energise without the medium that carries its heat away. Used within those limits, electric heating is reliable, compact, and fit for the narrow set of duties it does well.
