A heat pump is the most efficient way to heat with electricity because it does not make heat — it moves heat that already exists. Understanding it starts with seeing that it is simply a refrigeration cycle pointed in a useful direction.

## A refrigeration cycle run in reverse

Every refrigeration system already pumps heat uphill. A refrigerator takes heat from the cold food compartment and rejects it to the warm kitchen. An air conditioner takes heat from the room and rejects it outdoors. In both cases heat flows from cold to hot, against its natural direction, and a compressor does the work that makes that possible.

A **heat pump** is the same machine, but we care about the warm end instead of the cold end. The four components never change: an **evaporator** that absorbs heat at low temperature, a **compressor** that raises the refrigerant pressure and temperature, a **condenser** that rejects heat at high temperature, and an **expansion device** that drops the pressure again. In cooling mode the condenser is the hot side we ignore. In heating mode we deliver the condenser heat to the building and let the evaporator pull heat from outside.

The key insight: for every unit of electricity the compressor consumes, three or four or more units of heat are delivered, because most of that heat was harvested from the source rather than created.

## Air source water source and ground source

Heat pumps are classified by where the evaporator gets its heat.

| Type | Heat source | Source temperature stability | Typical heating COP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Air source | Outdoor air | Varies with weather | 2.5 to 4.0 |
| Water source | A water loop, well or cooling tower | Moderately stable | 3.5 to 5.0 |
| Ground source | Buried pipe loop in soil | Very stable | 4.0 to 5.5 |

**Air source** units are cheapest and simplest — the evaporator is a finned coil with a fan, mounted outdoors. Performance falls as the outdoor air gets cold because there is less heat to harvest and a bigger temperature lift to overcome.

**Water source** units take heat from a circulating water loop. In a large building a single loop can run at a steady temperature while some heat pumps reject heat into it and others draw heat out, balancing the building load.

**Ground source** units exchange heat with the soil through buried pipework. A few metres down the ground stays close to the annual average air temperature all year, so the source is warm in winter and cool in summer — which is why ground source delivers the highest and steadiest efficiency, at the highest installation cost.

## COP and how source temperature changes it

**COP**, the coefficient of performance, is heat delivered divided by electricity consumed. A COP of 4 means 4 kW of heat for every 1 kW of electricity.

COP is not a fixed number. It depends on the **temperature lift** — the gap between the source temperature at the evaporator and the delivery temperature at the condenser. A small lift gives a high COP; a large lift gives a low one.

- A warmer source raises COP, because the evaporator works at a higher temperature.
- A cooler delivery target raises COP, which is why heat pumps pair so well with low-temperature emitters such as underfloor heating and oversized fan coils.

This is the practical rule: keep the lift small. An air source heat pump delivering 35 °C water on a mild day might reach COP 4.5; the same unit delivering 55 °C water on a cold day might fall to COP 2.0.

## Reversing valve and defrost

A dedicated heating-only heat pump is rare. Most units are **reversible**, switching between heating and cooling with a single component called the **four-way reversing valve**. The valve redirects refrigerant flow so the indoor coil and outdoor coil swap roles — one machine, two seasons.

Air source units face one extra problem. When the outdoor coil runs below freezing while pulling heat from humid air, moisture condenses on it and freezes into frost. Frost insulates the coil and chokes airflow, so the unit periodically runs a **defrost cycle**: it briefly reverses to cooling mode, sending hot refrigerant through the outdoor coil to melt the ice, then returns to heating. Defrost costs a little energy and momentarily interrupts heat delivery, which is a normal and designed behaviour.

## The most efficient electric heating

Compare the options for heating with electricity. An electric resistance heater is about 100 percent efficient — one unit of electricity gives one unit of heat. A heat pump with a seasonal COP of 3.5 gives three and a half units of heat for that same unit of electricity. For the same comfort it uses roughly a third of the electricity, with a third of the running cost and a third of the associated carbon.

That advantage is why heat pumps are central to building decarbonisation. Where a clean electricity supply is available, a heat pump turns electric heating from the most expensive option into one of the cheapest to run.

> 🇬🇭 **Ghana context**
> Ghana is a hot climate, so comfort space heating is almost never needed and a reversible heat pump spends nearly all its life in cooling mode. Where heat pumps earn their place is as efficient water heaters — a heat pump water heater can deliver hot water at a third of the cost of an electric immersion element, a real saving against Ghana tariffs. They also suit the occasional process-heating or dehumidification reheat duty, where moving heat beats burning electricity outright.

## Practical selection points

When choosing a heat pump, match the source to the site, size it to the calculated load rather than a rule of thumb, and design the distribution for the lowest delivery temperature the emitters allow. Plan for a backup or supplementary heat source if the climate produces conditions colder than the unit's stable operating range, and confirm there is space and a route for condensate and defrost meltwater. The cleaner the design keeps the temperature lift, the more the machine rewards you.
