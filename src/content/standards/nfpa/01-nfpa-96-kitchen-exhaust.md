NFPA 96 is the document a designer, installer or inspector reaches for whenever a project includes commercial cooking. This study guide explains what the standard sets out to do and how to use it alongside the mechanical code.

## What NFPA 96 is

NFPA 96 is the **Standard for Ventilation Control and Fire Protection of Commercial Cooking Operations**, published and maintained by the National Fire Protection Association. It is a consensus fire-safety standard, updated on a regular revision cycle, that pulls together in one place every requirement for capturing, conveying and safely discharging the grease-laden air produced by commercial cooking — and for protecting the building if that grease ignites.

The standard exists because cooking exhaust is unlike any other duct system. The air carries condensable grease that coats the duct, and a hood or duct fire is one of the most common and most damaging fires in commercial buildings. NFPA 96 treats the whole assembly — hood, duct, fan, suppression and the cleaning regime — as a single fire-protection system.

## What the standard governs

NFPA 96 covers the commercial cooking exhaust system from the appliance to the point of discharge, broadly including:

- **Hoods** — construction, materials and the listed grease-extraction components, and the rules distinguishing grease-bearing exhaust from heat-and-steam exhaust.
- **Grease ducts** — welded liquid-tight construction, continuous slope so grease drains, dedicated routing, and the prohibition on any obstruction or damper within the duct.
- **Clearances and enclosures** — the separation required between grease ducts and combustible construction, and the listed enclosure and wrap systems that allow that clearance to be reduced.
- **Exhaust fans** — grease-rated fan construction, hinged or removable for cleaning, with grease containment, and discharge located clear of intakes and walkways.
- **Air movement and make-up air** — the requirement to replace exhausted air so the kitchen does not go excessively negative.
- **Fire suppression** — listed automatic systems protecting the hood, plenum, duct and cooking surfaces, with interlocks that cut fuel and power to the appliances on activation.
- **Cleaning and inspection** — periodic inspection of the system and cleaning of grease build-up at a frequency matched to the cooking volume, with records kept.

The standard also addresses solid-fuel cooking, recirculating systems, and the use of listed factory-built grease duct products, each with its own additional provisions. This guide describes the content in general terms only; consult the current edition for the precise section numbers and the exact requirements that apply to your project.

## How it works with the mechanical code

NFPA 96 does not stand alone. On most projects the legally adopted document is the **mechanical code** — the International Mechanical Code in jurisdictions that adopt it — and that code's chapter on commercial kitchen exhaust either repeats the core grease-duct and hood requirements or references NFPA 96 directly.

| Document | Role |
|---|---|
| Mechanical code | The adopted, enforceable law for the jurisdiction |
| NFPA 96 | The detailed fire-safety standard, often referenced by the code |
| Fire code and AHJ | Local amendments, permits and acceptance |

In practice the two are consistent on the fundamentals — Type I hoods over grease-producing appliances, welded liquid-tight grease ducts, no dampers in grease ducts, fire suppression on the cooking line. Where they differ in detail, the more restrictive provision and the authority having jurisdiction govern. The safe working approach is to design to NFPA 96, then check the locally adopted mechanical and fire codes for amendments and for the specific edition in force.

## Why it matters for a commercial kitchen project

Any project with a commercial kitchen — restaurant, hotel, hospital, school, staff canteen — falls under these requirements, and the consequences of ignoring them are serious:

- **Life safety.** A grease fire in an unprotected duct spreads quickly through the building. Correct construction, clearances and suppression are what contain it.
- **Code compliance.** A kitchen exhaust system that does not meet the standard will fail inspection, and the kitchen cannot legally open.
- **Insurance.** Insurers commonly require documented compliance and a current cleaning record; a fire in a non-compliant or uncleaned system can void a claim.
- **Operational cost.** A system designed to the standard — proper slope, accessible cleanout panels, a hinged fan — is far cheaper to clean and maintain over its life.

For the design engineer, the practical takeaways are consistent with the kitchen exhaust design article: select the right hood type, build a dedicated welded grease duct with no dampers, slope it to drain, keep listed clearances, fit a grease-rated upblast fan and tempered make-up air, protect the line with interlocked wet-chemical suppression, and set up a documented cleaning schedule. NFPA 96 is the reference that ties all of those decisions together.

## How to study it

Read NFPA 96 by following the air path. Start at the hood and its classification, move to the duct and its construction and clearance rules, then the fan and discharge, then the suppression system and its interlocks, and finally the inspection and cleaning provisions. Cross-reference each topic with the kitchen exhaust design article in this library, and always confirm the edition adopted in your jurisdiction before quoting a requirement on a drawing or in a submittal.
